Both tools give you a visual canvas, a CMS and hosting under one subscription, priced per site rather than per workspace. Where they differ is what actually moves the bill: on Webflow it's bandwidth and per-editor seats; on Framer, the plan's own sticker price can change depending on an AI-credit tier picked from a dropdown on the same plan card. Every figure below comes from Webflow's and Framer's own pricing pages and help centers.

## What does each plan actually include at the entry price?

Webflow's cheapest plan with a custom domain, Basic, costs <Price tool="webflow" plan="basic" /> a month and has no CMS at all: no blog, no Collections. A site that needs a blog has to move up to Premium, <Price tool="webflow" plan="premium" /> a month, which then includes 20,000 CMS items across 40 Collections. Framer's cheapest plan, Basic, costs <Price tool="framer" plan="basic" /> a month and already includes a small CMS: 2 Collections and 1,000 items, fixed with no overage. That $15 is only the price at Basic's lowest AI-credit tier, though. The same plan card lets you pick a higher tier from a dropdown, and picking one raises the whole plan's price on the spot, up to $225 a month at Basic's top tier, or up to $1,500 a month on Pro. Nothing on Webflow's side works that way; Premium's price is flat no matter how much you use Webflow's own AI credits, which are sold as a separate add-on instead.

## Where does Framer actually win?

Framer wins on cost per seat and on getting a CMS-capable site live for less. Its Basic plan undercuts Webflow's CMS plan by a wide margin, <Price tool="framer" plan="basic" /> against Webflow Premium's <Price tool="webflow" plan="premium" /> a month, while already including a small CMS that Webflow's matching Basic plan doesn't have at all. Adding people costs less too: a second Framer editor is $30 a month for full access or $15 a month for a content-only Content Editor seat, both cheaper than Webflow's $45 Full seat or $19 Limited seat. The first editor is free on both tools, but Framer's $30/$15 rate is the same on Basic or Pro, where Webflow's per-seat price depends only on the role, not the Site plan. Framer also builds AI design and content agents into every plan, able to draft a page or copy from a prompt, lowering how much design skill a solo owner needs before publishing.

## Where does Webflow actually win?

Webflow wins on ceiling and on not capping how many people can work on a site. There's no limit on paid editor seats: an agency can keep adding $19 Limited seats, where Framer caps a single workspace at 10 total editors on Basic and Pro alike, and only a custom Enterprise quote removes it. Webflow's Agency and Freelancer Workspaces, <Price tool="webflow" plan="agency-workspace" /> and <Price tool="webflow" plan="freelancer-workspace" /> a month, are also built specifically for running many client sites, with free client seats attached to each individual site rather than shared across one workspace-wide cap. Raw plan limits run higher too: Premium's 300 pages and 20,000 CMS items dwarf Basic's fixed 30 pages and 1,000 items on Framer, and Webflow Cloud lets a Premium site host a small web app, up to 2 million requests a month, alongside the marketing pages, with no Framer equivalent. Webflow's refund window is wider as well: any new Site or Workspace purchase is refundable within 14 days, against Framer's 7 days outside the EU and Turkey.

Read Framer's number as understated, not as the full picture.

## What does it cost to leave, and does either lock you in?

Neither platform is self-hosted, so leaving either one is a real project, not a setting you flip. Webflow gives you a partial way out: any paid Workspace plan, not the free Starter Workspace, can export a site's HTML and CSS, and CMS content exports one Collection at a time, though forms and any server-side logic stop working the moment you leave. Framer offers no equivalent: its own help center runs an article titled 'Can I export my website to HTML and self-host it?', and the answer is no, because a published Framer site depends on Framer's own backend rather than shipping as a portable static file. Billing itself doesn't lock you in on either side by default: both run month to month unless you choose annual billing. The one hard commitment sits at the top of Webflow's price list: the <Price tool="webflow" plan="team" field="annualMonthly" /> a month Team plan requires an annual contract with no monthly option, sold through Webflow's sales team. Framer's closest equivalent, Enterprise, is quote-only with no published price. Direction matters too: moving a Framer site from annual back to monthly billing isn't self-serve, it needs a support request, and any unused annual value converts to non-refundable account credit.

## Where most advice gets this wrong

Most comparisons stop at sticker price or a feature checklist, missing whether the quoted number is the whole bill. Framer's growing-team figure, <Real tool="framer" size="growing" /> a month, looks dramatically cheaper than Webflow's <Real tool="webflow" size="growing" />, and most of that gap is real. But part of it is an artifact of what each vendor lets you estimate. Webflow forces the bandwidth question into the open: it publishes a page-view range for every tier, so a bill like this one can price in a specific add-on for an estimated traffic level. Framer publishes no such range at all, so the same traffic level simply isn't priced into its number, not because Framer is cheaper at that load, but because there's no published way to convert visits into a bandwidth charge. The honest reading isn't "Framer costs a third of Webflow at scale"; it's "Framer's published price stops accounting for bandwidth at exactly the point Webflow's starts to." Anyone budgeting for a high-traffic site on Framer's Pro plan should track actual bandwidth from day one rather than assume the plan price is final, since Pro's overage rate, $50 for every extra 100 GB, applies even though no one, including Framer, publishes what a given visit count will use.

## What do the two "neither" verdicts actually mean?

Two of the picks below deserve a caveat. A business built around a real product catalog, the shopify-store persona, fits neither tool: Framer has no cart or checkout of any kind, and Webflow's Ecommerce plans cap out at 500 to 15,000 items with a transaction fee on the entry tier. Course creators land on "neither" for a sharper reason: neither tool can take a payment at all, for a course, a download or anything else, so the actual sale has to run through a separate platform regardless of which one builds the marketing pages in front of it. Read that verdict as "pick whichever building experience you prefer for the free content," not as a real gap between the two, and see the [course-creator stack](/stacks/course-creator/) for what handles the sale. Everyone else in the verdicts is choosing between two real site builders; the deciding factor is usually team size against Framer's 10-seat cap, or whether the business already has someone comfortable in a code-capable canvas.
