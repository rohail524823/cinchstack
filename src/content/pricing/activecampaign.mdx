ActiveCampaign's pricing page shows one number per plan and contact tier, and every single one of them carries the same small label: "Billed annually." What it leaves out, and what a buyer should know before committing a year's payment upfront, is the rest of this page.

## Which ActiveCampaign plan actually fits your contact count?

ActiveCampaign prices four plans, Starter, Plus, Pro and Enterprise, by how many contacts sit on the account, not by how many people use it. A solo operator emailing around 1,000 contacts a few times a month fits Starter, which caps out at one user seat and five actions per automation but still ships the same CRM and automation engine as every other plan. A small team running roughly 10,000 contacts still sits inside Starter's 25,000-contact ceiling, so the small-team bill above stays on the cheapest plan rather than forcing an upgrade. Past that, the choice usually turns on seats and automation depth rather than contacts: Plus removes Starter's automation-action cap and adds landing pages, and Pro raises the included seats from one to three and adds advanced segmentation and attribution reporting, useful once more than one person is building campaigns.

## What actually happens once your list outgrows the plan you're on?

ActiveCampaign's pricing-page FAQ states plainly that crossing into a higher contact band moves your account to the next band, not a penalty or a lockout, so a growing list alone never gets an account suspended or a feature switched off. The less obvious limit is how often you email that list, not just its size. ActiveCampaign's own plan-comparison table caps monthly email sends at a multiple of contact count: 10 times contacts on Starter and Plus, 12 times on Pro, and 15 times on Enterprise. A 50,000-contact list emailed roughly twelve times a month adds up to about 600,000 sends, past the 500,000 that Plus's allowance covers at that size and right at the 600,000 Pro's allowance provides instead. ActiveCampaign has not published whether that multiplier is an enforced cap or a looser guideline, so the growing-team bill above is recorded on Plus, the cheapest plan that covers 50,000 contacts. A business that actually sends to its full list that often should budget for <Price tool="activecampaign" plan="pro-50000" field="annualMonthly" /> a month on Pro instead, the safer real-world pick if that send limit turns out to be strictly enforced.

## Why does "billed annually" matter more than the sticker price?

There is no monthly/annual toggle anywhere on ActiveCampaign's pricing page, and the number displayed for every plan is not a price you can get by paying month to month; it is only what a year of service works out to per month once you have paid for the whole year. Multiplied out, the commitment gets clearer: Starter at 1,000 contacts comes to $180 for the year, and the growing-team bill above, on Plus at 50,000 contacts, comes to $7,308 for the year. ActiveCampaign's billing help article, visible only after you sign up, confirms that a genuine month-to-month cadence exists on the account, alongside a third option called "commit annually, pay monthly," but it names a price for neither one. That leaves a prospective buyer with no way to compare the true cost of paying monthly against the annual figure ActiveCampaign advertises everywhere else, which is the single biggest catch on this pricing page.

## What else can add to the bill beyond the plan price?

Setup is free on every plan, per ActiveCampaign's own pricing FAQ. Past that, almost everything optional has no public price at all. Starter includes one seat and allows one purchased extra; Plus, Pro and Enterprise include more seats and can add further ones, but ActiveCampaign has never published what an extra seat costs on any plan. The same is true of its enhanced-CRM add-ons, Pipelines and Sales Engagement, both sold on Plus and above; of SMS credits, also sold on Plus and above; and of the Custom Reports and Transactional Email add-ons available more broadly across the lineup. ActiveCampaign's Help Center confirms all of them exist and states which plans qualify to buy each one, but the actual charge only appears inside the account after you have already signed up and started the purchase. Treat the plan price as a floor, not the full bill, until you can check each add-on yourself while logged in.

## Is the 14-day trial enough to decide, and what if it isn't?

ActiveCampaign has no permanent free plan; its only no-cost option is a 14-day trial that needs no credit card and runs on Pro-plan features, so a trial account can test advanced segmentation and attribution reporting even if Starter or Plus is the plan you would actually buy. ActiveCampaign's Help Center adds that the Sales Engagement add-on and a Salesforce integration can be tested during that same window. What neither the pricing page nor the Help Center says is what happens to an account, its contacts and its automations once the 14 days end without a paid plan in place. If you do buy and the plan turns out to be wrong, the real safety net is the 30-day money-back guarantee on paid plans, not the trial, since it gives a full refund window that runs well past what 14 free days can show you. A business that wants to stay on a free tool indefinitely under 1,000 contacts will not find one here; <T id="kit" /> publishes a permanent free tier that covers up to 10,000 contacts, well past that mark, while <T id="mailchimp" />'s free tier stops at just 250 contacts, too small for most businesses approaching 1,000. Neither one bundles a CRM the way ActiveCampaign does.
