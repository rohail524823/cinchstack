A brand that sells physical products online needs software to do three jobs: take payment and ship orders, get customers to come back and buy again, and keep the people running the business from losing track of suppliers, SOPs and the catalog as it grows. That is the shape of the stack below: an ecommerce platform, an email and SMS tool built for retention, and a workspace for the operational detail the first two were never built to hold. A generic CRM, a second website builder and a full SEO platform aren't in it, and the sections below explain why.

## Which Shopify plan fits a store with no employees yet?

<Real tool="shopify" size="solo" /> a month buys the Basic plan, which covers a solo founder completely: unlimited products, hosting, a PCI-compliant checkout, abandoned-cart recovery, discount codes and automatic sales tax calculation, all in one bill. The catch is staff accounts. Basic includes none beyond the store owner, so the day the business hires even one person who needs their own login, the plan that actually fits is Grow, at <Real tool="shopify" size="small" /> a month for up to five staff accounts. A 15-person operation with several people who need admin access needs Advanced, at <Real tool="shopify" size="growing" /> a month, which raises the staff cap to 15 and includes third-party carrier-calculated shipping that Grow only gets with yearly billing or an extra monthly fee. None of this changes if the business also sells at a market or a pop-up: every paid plan covers casual in-person selling, so the numbers above hold either way.

## Is WooCommerce ever the better starting point for a physical-product brand?

On paper, WooCommerce is the cheaper start: <Real tool="woocommerce" size="solo" /> a month, almost all of it hosting, since the plugin itself costs nothing. That number only holds up if you or someone you pay is comfortable running WordPress: choosing a host, keeping the site backed up and updating plugins are your job on WooCommerce, not a vendor's, and WooCommerce's own pricing page is upfront that the real bill is hosting plus extensions plus payments, not one number. A physical-product brand with no in-house WordPress experience usually loses more time to that upkeep than the monthly difference saves. WooCommerce becomes the better pick when a brand already runs a WordPress site for a blog or a content audience and is adding a store to it, or wants full control of its checkout code with no share of revenue taken by the platform itself. For the full breakdown of how the two diverge as a store scales, see [Shopify vs WooCommerce](/compare/shopify-vs-woocommerce/).

## Why put email and SMS ahead of a CRM?

The single highest-leverage purchase after the store itself is the tool that emails and texts people who already bought something, not a system for logging sales calls. Klaviyo's flows read a customer's real order and browsing history from Shopify or WooCommerce, so an abandoned-cart or back-in-stock message goes out already knowing what that person looked at, and its SMS rate is published rather than something you discover after signing up. <Real tool="klaviyo" size="solo" /> a month for a first list costs more than doing nothing and less than most other places that money could go at this stage. A CRM such as <T id="hubspot" /> or <T id="gohighlevel" /> solves a different problem: moving a deal through stages until someone signs a contract. A store selling physical products to consumers doesn't run that workflow, and the customer data a CRM would hold already lives in the platform's order history and the email tool's segments.

## What keeps a growing catalog from turning into supplier chaos?

A solo founder's first real operational risk isn't a missed sale, it's forgetting which supplier makes which SKU or shipping the wrong box dimensions after a packaging change. <Real tool="notion" size="solo" /> a month buys exactly that: unlimited pages for one person, with no cap on total storage the way a free project-management tool's fixed workspace-wide limit would hit fast once product photos and spec sheets pile up. The catch is that Notion's free plan still caps each individual file at 5 MB, so a full-resolution product photo sometimes needs resizing first. A five-person team removes that per-file cap outright, with unlimited file uploads and guest access for a freelance photographer or a fulfillment partner, at <Real tool="notion" size="small" /> a month, and a 15-person team stays on the same plan at <Real tool="notion" size="growing" /> a month, since Notion publishes no seat cap that would force an upgrade at this size. <T id="clickup" /> is worth switching to instead of, not because of, growing headcount: once restock timing, supplier purchase orders and launch dates are moving across several people at once, its Gantt and timeline views and workflow automations fit that coordination better than a wiki does.

## Do you need a separate website or SEO platform once the store is live?

A live store on Shopify or WooCommerce already renders its own product pages, collection pages and blog, so a second site on <T id="webflow" /> or <T id="squarespace" /> means paying for, and keeping in sync, two places that both claim to be the storefront. The same logic applies to search visibility: a catalog's biggest SEO gains usually come from writing real product titles, descriptions and image alt text, not from a keyword-tracking platform built for a content team publishing dozens of articles a month. Two free tools cover what a small catalog actually needs before that: Google Search Console shows which searches already bring buyers to specific product pages, and Google Merchant Center lists products in Google's free shopping results, with an official app on both Shopify and WooCommerce that keeps the feed synced automatically.

## Where most advice gets this wrong

Most "best stack for your online store" lists give every category equal weight: a CRM slot, an SEO slot, a page-builder slot, next to the ecommerce platform, as if a solo founder and a 15-person team faced the same stakes in each box. They don't. Shopify runs <Real tool="shopify" size="solo" /> to <Real tool="shopify" size="growing" /> a month across the sizes on this page; Klaviyo runs <Real tool="klaviyo" size="solo" /> to <Real tool="klaviyo" size="growing" /> over that same range, and Klaviyo's own "profile compliance" rule moves an account to a pricier tier automatically once its list outgrows the current one. The category worth watching as this business grows isn't the platform tier; it's the one priced by list size, which keeps climbing whether or not anyone suppresses the contacts who stopped opening email months ago.
