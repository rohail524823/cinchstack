## What does a freelancer selling services actually need running?

A freelancer selling their own time or expertise directly to a client is running a different business than a course creator or an agency, and that changes what "professional" and "get paid" actually mean here: a client needs to see a real portfolio and a clean invoice, not a checkout page or a funnel built for a crowd of buyers. The stack below covers that with three tools doing three jobs: a site that also sends the invoice, a place to track who you're talking to before they're a client, and somewhere client notes and proposals actually live. It skips a dedicated email platform and a CRM built for an agency running campaigns across many clients at once, because neither fits a business built around one person's own work.

## Can one website really cover your portfolio and your invoices?

Yes, and for most freelancers it should. <T id="squarespace" /> costs <Price tool="squarespace" plan="basic" /> a month on its Basic plan, which covers a solo operator outright: up to 1,000 pages, unlimited bandwidth, and a built-in invoicing tool that carries no Squarespace fee on any plan, on top of the site itself. The one hard limit is contributors: Basic caps at two, the owner included, so the moment you bring in help to run the site, the plan that actually fits is Core, at <Price tool="squarespace" plan="core" /> a month, which removes the cap entirely and keeps the site's cost flat well past a five-person team.

<T id="wix" /> is the cheaper-looking alternative worth naming directly: its Light plan runs <Price tool="wix" plan="light" field="annualMonthly" /> a month, Wix's only published rate, and still gets you a real custom domain with no Wix branding. The catch most comparisons skip is that Light excludes online payments, customer accounts and the entire eCommerce toolkit outright, so you'd still need a separate way to invoice. <T id="framer" /> is the pick when the site itself has to read as a design portfolio rather than a service brochure, though it has no built-in invoicing either, so you'd pair it with a way to get paid the same way Wix's Light plan needs one. Squarespace's real advantage for this business isn't the templates; it's that the invoice and the site come out of the same subscription.

## Do you need a CRM before you have a pipeline to manage?

Not much of one, and definitely not an expensive one. <T id="zoho-crm" /> is free for up to three users, no card required, and it already does what a solo consultant's pipeline actually needs: track who you've talked to, when you followed up, and what stage a proposal is at, with workflow automation to remind you. That covers a freelancer working alone and a freelancer with two contractors, without paying anything.

The standard reason a CRM's price jumps at this size is mass email: run a real marketing-email volume through most CRMs and the free tier stops qualifying. A consulting pipeline doesn't run that way. You're emailing individual prospects and clients, not blasting a list, and Zoho's free plan already allows up to 50 one-to-one emails a day per user, plenty for that job. The bill only moves once a fourth person needs a seat, because Free is hard-capped at exactly three users with no way to add a fourth: at that size, Standard costs <Price tool="zoho-crm" plan="standard" /> per user a month, still with no mass-email feature you'd actually use.

<T id="pipedrive" /> is worth the extra cost for one specific reason: e-signed proposals. Its Lite plan starts at <Price tool="pipedrive" plan="lite" /> a month, but sending a proposal a client can sign online needs the Smart Docs add-on on top, which Pipedrive only bundles free once you're on its far pricier Premium plan. If closing a deal genuinely runs through a signed document rather than an emailed PDF and a reply, that's the one case worth paying for over Zoho's free plan.

## Where do proposals, client notes and SOPs actually live?

Nowhere expensive, at least at first. <T id="notion" /> is free and unlimited in pages and blocks for a single-person workspace, which covers a solo consultant's client notes, meeting recaps, proposal drafts and process docs with no seat fee at all. The catch shows up the moment a second person joins: Free becomes "limited" for any workspace with two or more members, and Notion doesn't publish what that cap actually is, so the safer move once you add a contractor is Plus, at <Price tool="notion" plan="plus" /> per member a month, which removes the cap along with the 5 MB file-upload limit and the 7-day page history Free is stuck with.

<T id="clickup" /> is the better fit once you're not just sharing docs but actually assigning work: its free plan has no time limit and no seat cap, and its paid plans add permission-controlled guest seats, so a client can see task status without editing everything or needing a paid seat of their own. Move to it, or add it alongside Notion, once deadlines and deliverables need to be tracked and assigned across more than just you, not before. ## Where most advice gets this wrong

Most software round-ups for freelancers treat the CRM or the "practice management" platform as the centerpiece purchase, the way an all-in-one platform like <T id="gohighlevel" /> or <T id="hubspot" /> is for an agency. That's backwards for a one-person service business. A client never logs into your CRM, never opens your project tracker, and never knows or cares which one you picked. They see two things: the site that made them trust you enough to reach out, and the invoice that decides whether paying you is easy or annoying. Spend on those two, and treat everything else as back-office plumbing that should cost as little as possible.

That's the actual case for pairing a real website budget, <T id="squarespace" />'s <Price tool="squarespace" plan="basic" /> a month, with a free CRM and a free workspace tool, rather than the reverse most guides suggest: a bare-bones site builder next to a paid "sales platform." The whole stack for a solo consultant runs <Stack id="freelancer" size="solo" /> a month this way, almost all of it going toward the one thing a client actually judges you on.

## What changes once you're not working alone anymore?

The bill grows in the two layers built around headcount, not in the site. Add two contractors and Notion needs its Plus plan the moment there's a second person in the workspace, while Zoho CRM stays free right up until a fourth person needs a login, since Free is capped at exactly three users with no way to add a fourth. A freelancer with two contractors runs the stack for about <Stack id="freelancer" size="small" /> a month; the site itself doesn't move, because Core was already priced for more contributors than that.
